# SAMPLE PAIR — THE DEVICE, DEMONSTRATED
## Chapter Eight territory. You at twelve. Him at twelve.

**What this is:** a proof that the cut works on the page, not finished prose. The
architecture is what I'm handing you. The voice is a placeholder for yours — every
line of the D sections should be overwritten in your own mouth. I've kept them
short on purpose so there's room.

**What it obeys:** no interiority claimed for Him. Nothing invented that isn't
already in Luke 2. His section stays mundane and stays under a page. The note He
writes is a fragment, not a teaching. The mechanism is never explained.

## PART ONE — the cut out

*(end of your section — you at twelve, the street)*

> By twelve I had a system.
>
> Weight in the crawlspace, not the house. Money in three places so no one place
> could hurt me. I knew which grown men would front me and which ones were
> waiting to catch me short. I knew which of them looked at me a half-second too
> long when I walked in — and I learned to read that look before I could read a
> paragraph without losing my place.
>
> I told myself I was getting money.
>
> I was not getting money. I was standing in front of every man in that parish who
> would let me stand there, waiting to be told I did good.
>
> That's what I was buying. I just didn't have the words for it yet.

## PART TWO — the cut to Him

*(this is the whole Jesus section. It does not get longer than this.)*

> Somewhere in the same twelfth year, a boy sat down at a table in Nazareth.
>
> He had been missing three days. His mother had walked back to Jerusalem with a
> fear I don't have to imagine, because I have watched my own mother carry it. When
> they found Him He was in the Temple, and what He said to them was that He had to
> be in His Father's house.
>
> Then Luke says something plain and easy to walk past. He went home with them. He
> was obedient to them. And His mother kept all these things in her heart.
>
> She kept them. That is the word. She held onto them so they would not be lost.
>
> So here is the part nobody wrote down, and I am telling you plainly that nobody
> wrote it down. It's evening. The work is finished. There is a broken piece of a
> water jar on the table, the kind everybody used when they had something to keep
> and nothing to keep it on. He has a scrap of charcoal from the fire.
>
> I imagine it felt like something He did not want to lose.
>
> He writes seven words on the shard.
>
> *No father hands his son a stone.*
>
> Then He puts it somewhere, and He goes to bed, and He gets up the next morning
> and goes to work, and He does that for eighteen more years.

## PART THREE — the seed surfacing

*(back on your side. Placement is the whole trick — this does not go in Chapter
Eight. It surfaces later, thirty years later, in the chapters that haven't been
written yet. I'm putting it here only so you can see the shape of the return.)*

> It came to me in a cell, and I want to be careful about how I say this, because I
> am not claiming I heard a voice.
>
> I did not hear anything.
>
> I remembered something.
>
> A father doesn't hand his son a stone. That's the whole thing. It arrived finished,
> the way your own phone number arrives, and I sat there turning it over trying to
> work out where I had picked it up, because I had never read it anywhere and nobody
> ever said it to me.
>
> And then it opened up.
>
> I spent from eleven years old to thirty-three asking the street to be my father.
> Every man I fronted for, every man I ran for, every man I took a charge for — I
> was walking up with my hands out asking for bread.
>
> And every one of them put a rock in my hand.
>
> That's what love does not do. That's how you know the difference. That's the
> whole test and I had it in me the entire time, sitting under thirty years of mud,
> waiting for somebody to finally get quiet enough to hear it.

## NOTES ON WHAT I DID AND WHY

**The double meaning on "rock" is the load-bearing move.** Bread and stone is His
image, from Luke 11:11 — *which of you fathers, if your son asks for bread, will
give him a stone?* On your side, a rock is a rock. The fragment He wrote at twelve
lands in your hand as literal contraband thirty years later. You don't explain the
pun. You let the reader step on it.

**Second payoff, and this is why the fragment has to be a fragment.** He writes
seven crude words at twelve. Eighteen years later that scrap grows into an actual
recorded saying in Luke 11. So the reader watches a teaching get *made* — sees the
seed of it on a broken jar in a kitchen — and then meets it fully grown in the
gospel. The device pays off twice: once in the gospels, once in you.

**The potsherd solves the paper problem.** Ostraca were the scrap paper of the
ancient world — broken pottery, free, everywhere, used by everyone including the
poor. It costs you nothing and it lets you keep Him writing.

**Luke 2:51 does the heavy lifting.** *His mother kept all these things in her
heart.* That verse is sitting directly after the Temple scene, in scripture,
attached to that exact household — the keeping-so-it-isn't-lost motif, already
canonical. You're not building the device. You're pointing at it.

**"I am not claiming I heard a voice. I remembered something."** That sentence is
the whole thesis of the device and it should appear exactly once in the book. It
converts the entire mechanism from a revelation claim into a memory claim, which is
a fraction of the ask, and it's the version a skeptical reader can stay in the room
for.

**What I deliberately did not do:** explain the seed, name the doctrine, or connect
the dots for the reader. The first two or three of these pass with no commentary at
all. This one has a little because it's the demonstration. In the book, the
explanation comes once, late, after the reader has already assembled it.

## WHAT THIS TELLS YOU ABOUT CHAPTERS NINE AND TEN

Your correction — JCCY and Bridge City are both LTI, one stint, processing then the
nightmare — has a structural consequence worth deciding now.

Chapter Ten currently carries the whole incarceration: intake at JCCY, transfer to
Bridge City, and walking out a trained weapon. That's three distinct movements in
one chapter, and the middle one is the one you called the nightmare.

Two options:

**A. Split it.** Nine ends at sentencing. Ten is JCCY — the processing floor, Angola
for kids, learning the rules. Eleven is Bridge City. Twelve is the walk-out. That
gives the worst stretch its own room and it gives you three Jesus pairings across
ages 13-16 instead of one.

**B. Keep it whole and pay for it.** One chapter, three movements, on the argument
that compression *is* the experience — that it all ran together at the time and the
reader should feel that. Costs you the room to slow down where it hurt most.

I'd take A, and not because of pacing. Because the Jesus side at 13-16 is your
emptiest stretch — no scripture, no tradition, nothing — and this is where the
device earns its keep. Three short cuts to a boy working a trade in silence, set
against three escalating stages of a boy being manufactured into a weapon, is the
best use of the imbalance you already decided to build the book on.
