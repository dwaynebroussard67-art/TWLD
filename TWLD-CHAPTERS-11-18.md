CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE DRIVEWAY AND THE DEEP WELL
In South Louisiana, the dark doesn't just fall; it settles. It is a heavy, wet blanket that smells of damp earth and the distant smoke of sugar cane fires.
When I finally walked out of the gladiator pit, I came home a violent man, a trained weapon searching for a love I didn't think existed, ready to fight anyone who stood in my way. But that concrete wasteland had left a mark on me. I knew the smell of the iron doors and the stale, desperate sweat of boys fighting for a stool. It scared me straight. I didn't know much about life at seventeen, but I knew I didn't want to end up back in a place where good things were choked to death.
I was trying to stay out of the fire, but my hands were still covered in gasoline.
I had just turned seventeen. I had my license, and I had just bought my own car. One afternoon, I was sitting in an outbuilding that belonged to our landlord, smoking weed. I wasn't trying to cut up. I just wanted a moment of quiet, a brief pause in the electric hum of cicadas that sounded like the world was about to boil over. But a spark caught. The dry wood went up, and the building burned to the dirt. The air turned thick with the smell of scorched timber and white ash.
When the authorities came, they bypassed me entirely. They dragged my brother out to take the fall.
I stood up. I looked the landlord in the eye and broke the silence. "No. It wasn't him. It was me."
The landlord turned to my mother and laid down the consequence. “I don't care what happens, but he has to leave. You can stay, but if your son lives with you, you have to pack your things.”
My mama had already paid enough for my existence. I wasn't going to let her pack a bag because of a fire I started. I gathered my things that afternoon.
For the next six weeks, I was homeless. I lived entirely out of the front seat of that car. I didn't park in my mother's driveway. Sometimes, I would park on the corner down the street, sitting in the dark just to watch her house and make sure she was safe. But mostly, I just slept wherever I put the car in park.
It is a strange, suffocating thing to sleep in a car in the Louisiana heat. The vinyl seats stick to your skin, and the air inside gets so thick you have to chew it before you can swallow it. As I lay there in the dark, watching the streetlights cut through the windshield, I started to understand the mechanics of my own fear.
I have never been afraid of physical pain. I knew the scent of copper before I knew the scent of a Sunday dinner. You can split my skin, and the blood won't bother me. But a broken heart? The threat of abandonment? That terrified me down to the marrow of my bones.
I remembered holding my brother's hand in the tall, un-mowed fields, listening to the sound of my mother being beaten into the floorboards. I remembered the vow I made in those wet weeds: I swore that one day I'd be big enough that I would never run from anything I was afraid of ever again.
That vow had become the engine of my trauma. When the panic set in, I didn't retreat. I ran directly at the things that terrified me. I charged the fire because the feeling of fear was infinitely worse than the feeling of a fist.
I went back to the heavy bag. Every night, that bag became my altar. I wrapped my hands and beat the canvas until my knuckles bled and the leather smelled like stale sweat and iron. By the time I was eighteen, my hands were lethal. I wasn't looking to prey on anyone, but if any man brought the threat of fear to my door, I was fully prepared to break him.
But for the last three weeks of that homeless stretch, the geography of my life shifted. I stopped sleeping on random corners. I started parking in a specific driveway—the driveway of Jean and Dean Lovett.
This is where the world's rules finally broke. Because in a life where I was used to bleeding myself dry for people who took the cup and walked away, the Lovetts were the quiet, solid exception. They didn't leave me out there on the concrete to sweat in the dark. They invited me inside.
A misfit loves completely, bending and shaping themselves to fit the spaces others leave empty, hoping someone will finally give it back. Almost no one ever does. But Jean and Dean Lovett did. They were the rare kind of family that actually returned the loyalty you gave them.
Beneath the muscle and the violence, I was a rotting man pretending to be alive. There is no good in D. The flesh that wraps my bones is entirely corrupt. The only pieces of humanity I had inside my chest were fragments I had stolen—borrowed light from the very few who showed me what the King looked like.
Earl James Fritz was the first. And then, there was Mr. Dean.
I only had Mr. Dean in my life for a solid year and a half before he died. But in that brief, brilliant window, he changed the fundamental way I saw the world. He was a great man. He walked with a quiet strength that didn't require him to break people to prove it. He left such a staggering impact on my soul that years later, I would name my own son after him.
I didn't need decades with him. The time I had was enough to plant a seed in the deep well of my heart—a seed that the gladiator pits, the burning sheds, and the long nights in the car couldn't completely dig up.
