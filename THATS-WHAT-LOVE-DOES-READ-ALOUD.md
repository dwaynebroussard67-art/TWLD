## Introduction: The Tablet of My Heart

You do not pick up a book like this unless you already know the cold. I know it because I was born in it, and I spent the majority of my life trying to find a way out.
This is the mind, the life, and the heart of a misfit. I spent decades never fitting in anywhere. I was constantly shape-shifting, trying to adapt to some invisible standard, never feeling comfortable in my own skin. I was misunderstood, harshly judged, and abandoned. And here is the brutal truth of my trench: every single scar on my heart was put there by somebody who looked me in the eye and swore they would never hurt me. Every deep cut came from someone who promised they were my safe place.
So I ran. I bounced from relationship to relationship, from crowd to crowd, hiding the dark and pretending I was normal. I disguised my survival tactics as self-improvement, or I tried to numb the isolation with whatever I could find. But that was an illusion. The raw truth was that my soul was dying. I was desperate for acceptance, but I was absolutely terrified that if anyone actually saw the real darkness inside of me, they would leave.
I fought alone, teeth gritted, refusing to ask for help—not out of bravery, but out of the bone-deep certainty that no human cavalry was coming. The world looked at me and called it stubbornness. I called it survival. I poured myself out, loving completely, praying against hope that just this one time, someone would finally give it back. But the Matrix cannot fix a misfit.
I couldn't be fixed. I could only be found.
And then, the King stepped into my dirt. Jesus Christ walked directly into the literal fire with me. For the first time in my life, I found someone who loved me for absolutely no reason. I had nothing good to offer Him. I was empty. But He saw the exact darkness I had spent my whole life hiding, and He did not flinch.
He brought the Khuba—an active, bleeding, sacrificial love. To a mind built on survival, a love with no price tag is terrifying. But the second I accepted it, I held onto it, and I will hold onto it until my bones break and my fingers bleed. He is the only one who didn't throw me away. Looking back down the long, violent road of my life, I realize He was the one loving me through the hands of random strangers when I was completely unlovable, keeping me breathing in the dark until I could meet Him face to face.
What you are holding in your hands is not just a book. You are reading the tablet of my heart. This is my perception of the world, written in the scars of a misfit, and redefined by what Jesus is teaching me. This is the truth about the trench, the rescue, and the King who pulled me out.
And that’s what love does.

## Introduction Part II: The Harrowing and the Flesh

There is nothing good in D.
Let me make that brutally, violently clear before you turn another page. The flesh that wraps my bones is as corrupt as a thing can be. Left to my own devices, I am selfish, broken, and built to ruin the things I touch. D is no good only. Every single ounce of good you might see in me, every right step I take, and every piece of truth written in this book is nothing but a mirror. It is only the Jesus Christ in me. I am a changed man today, but do not mistake the rescue for my own merit. The old man didn't fix himself; the King put him to death and gave me the Nura.
This book, That's What Love Does, is built on one unbreakable foundation. When I use the word "love," I am not talking about a feeling or the cheap imitation the world sells. I am talking about the Harrowing of Hell.
Jesus didn't just die to be a tragic martyr. He let the enemy kill Him. He let them drive the nails through His flesh and stop His heartbeat so He could become the ultimate Trojan horse. He let Himself be dragged to the absolute bottom of Hell for one specific reason: He went there to come get me.
He detonated the iron doors from the inside out. He walked into the darkest, most desolate pit of my isolation, looked at my chains, and broke them with His bare hands.
And still... He came for me. He stepped into my hell knowing exactly how wretched the flesh was. He saw the absolute rot inside of me, the exact depth of my rebellion, and the completely corrupt individual inhabiting this body. He knew exactly what kind of person He was bleeding for. And knowing all of that, He still allowed Himself to be brutally murdered. Just for me.
That is Khuba. It is an active, bleeding, sacrificial love that willingly steps into the absolute worst of human rot to pull a misfit out.
Every principle in this book, every lesson Abba is teaching me about The Code—Trust, Love, Respect, and Honesty—points back to that one violent act of rescue. Love isn't polite. Love doesn't stand on the edge of the pit and shout down advice to a rotting man. Love lets itself be killed to go to Hell and save the completely undeserving.
That is the standard. That is the baseline for everything that follows.
And that’s what love does.

## CHAPTER ONE: THE DESCENT

There are different kinds of dark.
There is the simple, honest dark of a night out in Abbeville when the moon is hidden behind the clouds and the air is thick with the damp heat of the South. That dark is just an absence of light. It is natural. It is resting.
But then there is a heavier dark. It is a thick, suffocating thing that settles into the corners of a room and the hollows of a man’s chest. It is the dark of the grave. It is the dark of a door that has been locked from the outside.
When they drove the iron through the King’s wrists on a Friday, the world thought they had finally locked that door on the Light. You can read it for yourself in the old letters of Peter and Paul. It isn’t a metaphor to make broken people feel better. It is a tactical reality. When the King’s flesh failed, the Matrix celebrated. The enemy looked at the blood drying on the wood and thought the story was over. Saturday was a day of terrible, heavy silence.
But beneath the earth, a storm was brewing. Ephesians 4 asks a blunt question: “What does ‘He ascended’ mean except that He also descended to the lower, earthly regions?” Theologians call it the Harrowing of Hell. I call it the ultimate infiltration. Jesus didn't just die to be a tragic martyr. He let the enemy kill Him so He could cross enemy lines. While the world above was quiet, the King was taking the keys to Death and Hades. He walked up to the iron doors of the underworld, looked at the absolute worst place in all of creation, and kicked the gates off their hinges. He went into the depths of my heart, because my heart was the darkest place in all of creation. It was hell. And He went there on purpose, to pull the captives out.
For thirty-four years, I didn't know anything about a tactical breach. I only knew the silence of the cell.
My descent wasn't a sudden, violent crash. The worst kind of breaking never is. It was a slow, creeping leak. It was a lifetime of shape-shifting, of trying to mold myself into whatever shape the world demanded, only to find that I never fit the cast. I gave myself away in pieces to people who promised they were a safe place. I poured out my heart, praying against hope that just once, someone would pour it back. But they only kept me around as long as I was useful. When the cup was empty, they threw it away.
You can only be abandoned so many times before the isolation stops being a feeling and becomes a physical place. I built a prison with my own hands and lived inside it. I walked around in the Louisiana sun, I smiled, I did the work, but my mind was buried underground.
Let me be brutally, violently honest with you before we go any further. There is no good in D. The flesh that wraps my bones is entirely corrupt. Left to my own devices, I am selfish, I am broken, and I am a vessel built for ruin. Every good thing you see in me, every piece of truth on these pages, is only the Jesus Christ in me. I was a rotting man pretending to be alive.
And eventually, a rotting man reaches the edge.
For me, the edge was not a poetic concept. It was a tangible, heavy reality in a quiet room. It was the rattle of a pill bottle. It was the rough fibers of a rope. It was the absolute, crushing certainty that no human cavalry was coming. I was entirely alone. I had reached the very bottom of my own personal hell, and I was ready to close the door and lock it from the inside to finally stop the bleeding.
Or so I thought.
What the dope fiend, the convict, and the broken do not realize in the dark is that the Harrowing did not end two thousand years ago. It is an ongoing rescue mission.
While I was staring at the end of my life, sitting in the thickest, heaviest dark I had ever known, the King was already making His descent. He didn't stand on a cloud and yell down advice. Advice is useless to a man holding a rope. Well-meaning people will try to fix you with their words, but you cannot fix a completely corrupt man. You can only find him.
And so, He did exactly what He did on that silent Saturday. He stepped directly into the dirt with me. Jesus Christ walked into the literal fire of my isolation. He brought the Nura into a room that hadn't seen light in decades. He looked at the completely ruined, hopeless, toxic man I was. He saw the full depth of my corruption, knowing exactly what kind of wretched flesh He was dealing with.
And He did not flinch.
He stood there in the dark and showed me what Khuba looks like. He loved me for absolutely no reason, asking nothing in return, offering a grace I could never earn. I had spent my entire life bleeding out for people who promised not to hurt me, but it was only at the absolute bottom of hell that I found the only King who actually keeps His word. He didn't just reach down to pull me out of the pit. He let Himself be killed to climb down into it, just so He could break my chains from the inside.
That is the Harrowing. That is the rescue of a misfit.
And that's what love does.

## CHAPTER TWO: THE STOLEN GARDEN

In the beginning, before the iron and the concrete, there was a garden.
The book of Genesis paints a picture of humanity in its purest state. Adam and Eve walked with God in the cool of the day.[1] There was no shame and no walls. That garden is the blueprint of a child's innocence—before the world introduces them to cruelty, they trust easily.
But I didn't get to keep my garden. My Eden was burned to the ground before I was old enough to understand what fire was.
Sin corrupted my story early. I was forced to witness things no child should ever see. And here is the heavy truth the King revealed to me: when a child is traumatized, the mind detaches from innocence. The flesh is too weak to process horror, so the soul is forced to age decades in a single night. You become a battle-scarred veteran trapped in a little boy’s frame.
That is the moment the misfit is born. You stand in a room of kids, but you are an alien. You look through ancient, tired eyes, knowing nobody understands the dark you carry. You are an outsider, cast out of Eden. And because no one understands the trauma, their advice sounds like static.
Jesus knew this. He looked at my shattered innocence and knew advice wouldn't work. He knew I couldn't conceptualize human love because, outside of my mother, love had only ever been a weapon used against me.
So He did the only thing a true King could do.
He didn't just wrap Himself in flesh; He stripped Himself for the breach. You can't kill God. The Matrix has no weapon that can stop the Divine. So, in a tactical move that defies human logic, Jesus took off His divinity.[2] He handed His power back to the Father in full so that He could become 100% man. He had to be able to bleed. He had to be able to break. He had to be able to die.
Before this, when He walked the earth in His power, He healed the sick and told them, "Your sins are forgiven."[3] He had the divine right to heal the spirit because all disease starts there. But the moment He laid that divinity down to submit to death, He no longer had the power to forgive sin on His own authority on earth. He had given it all back to the Father.
Imagine how hard they had to beat Him. If He were still standing there as 100% God, they could have struck Him for a thousand years and never stopped His heart. He had to shed that protection to meet us in the dirt. And when He was hanging there, beaten beyond recognition, He looked at the very people who were murdering Him—the first ones He had to see forgiven after laying down His power—and He didn't command their forgiveness. He begged for it.
When He cried out, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do,"[4] the critics and the detractors laughed. They thought it was proof He wasn't God because He was asking someone else for help. But the deeper truth, the Misfit truth, is that He had to ask because He had become exactly like us. He was a man in the fire, stripped of everything, using His final breaths to intercede for the people driving the nails.
He didn't just observe our trauma; He suffered it as a man. He took the horror into a human body that could feel every ounce of it. He did it so that when He stood before me in the dark, He didn't have to offer empty advice. He could look me in my tired eyes and say, "I know what it's like to be powerless. I know what it's like to be at the mercy of the corrupt. I know, because I laid down Heaven just to stand here with you."
He didn't just explain Khuba. He became the sacrifice that defined it.

## CHAPTER THREE: THE NIGHTMARE AND THE KINSMAN

There is a height a boy reaches where his fingers can just barely catch the cold brass of a doorknob. In South Louisiana, in the early 1980s, that height puts your eyes right at the level of the window screens—screens that were always gray with dust and salt, vibrating with the constant, electric hum of cicadas that sounded like the world was about to boil over.
My first memories are the smell of hot, summer dew on long grass and the way the light dies around 8:30. The dark here doesn’t just fall; it settles. It’s a heavy, wet blanket that smells of damp earth and the distant smoke of sugar cane fires. It’s an atmosphere that sticks to your skin and makes every breath feel like a chore. For me, that air was thick with the scent of fear.
People back then thought a kid could handle whatever the adults were doing. They didn't realize that a child who had already seen blood on the linoleum could no longer tell the difference between a movie and the truth. When I watched Freddy Krueger or the girl being dragged into the TV in Poltergeist, I didn't see Hollywood. I saw a confirmation of the world I already lived in. In the shadows of those small, humid rooms, monsters weren't a theory. They were the men who lived in the house.
I would tiptoe to my mama’s door, the floorboards creaking under my weight, my heart hammering like a trapped bird. I ground my teeth so hard in my sleep that I’d spit out pieces of enamel in the morning. I was two. I was three. I was a baby in a world that valued silence, where the neighbors' windows were close enough to touch, yet the walls were thick enough to hide the screaming.
I remember holding my brother’s hand in the tall, un-mowed fields. He was nine—a giant to me, but just a boy in a pair of hand-me-down jeans. We stood there with our little suitcases, shaking, listening to the sound of our mother being beaten into the floorboards of a house that should have been a sanctuary.
This was a neighborhood of kinfolk, people who lived within a stone’s throw. The early 80s here was a time of porch-sitting and heavy silence. Everyone knew the rhythm of the neighborhood, but when the screaming started, the atmosphere turned deaf. The help never came. The sprawling heritage around us felt like a cage where the bars were made of the very air we breathed—stagnant and unmoving.
I watched her get beat through the cracks of my hiding spots. I watched the person I loved most pay the price for our existence. And in those tall, wet weeds, I made a pact with the dark: I swore that one day I’d be big enough that I would never run from anything I was afraid of ever again. I didn't know then that the vow would turn me into a man who met every fear with a fist, or that I was being brainwashed by a man who used hunger and the locking of doors as his primary language.
I didn't want a daddy. If that was what a "man of the house" was in this heavy, silent air, I wanted to be an orphan.
While the Louisiana dew was soaking into my clothes and the cicadas were screaming in the trees, there was another Child growing up in a different time, under a different sun.
In the Taleheding traditions, we see a young Jesus walking the dusty paths of Nazareth. He was a boy who reached for doorknobs and felt the heat of the day, just like me. But in His house, the shadows didn't bite. He lived a life of staggering, quiet perfection. The atmosphere around Him wasn't heavy with the stench of secrets; it was clear and light.
Can you imagine a childhood where every hand extended was for healing? While I was learning that the world was a place of hunger and locked doors, He was growing in wisdom, becoming the perfect Mirror of a Father who never fails. He was the only Child in history who never had to hide in the grass. He never had to grind His teeth against the night terrors because He was the Light that the darkness couldn't touch.
He was the perfect Son, preparing to meet the world’s most imperfect sons.
He lived that perfection in the middle of a common, working-class life to forge a weapon. He was becoming the only Lamb who could eventually walk into my tall grass, sit down next to a shaking boy with a suitcase, and say, "I have lived the Life you were denied, so that I can give you the Father you never had

## CHAPTER FOUR: THE BREATH AND THE BLADE

There is a certain kind of quiet that only exists on a Saturday morning in the South. It’s a deceptive thing. It feels like peace, but for us, it was just the pause between heartbeats. We were a pack of four—Troy, Chastity, G.R., and me. We didn't need words. We had a language of looks and shadows, a secret code born of a war that the rest of the world couldn't see.
I was six when the Oldsmobile pulled into the drive. It was early, the kind of hour where the dew is still heavy enough to soak your shoes. I jumped into the front seat, standing tall to reach my mama’s height, and I reached out to hug her.
But I didn't smell home.
Before I even touched her, the air changed. At six years old, I was already well-trained, a tiny bloodhound for violence. I knew the scent of copper before I knew the scent of a Sunday dinner. The closer I got to her, the stronger it hit me—that metallic, sharp sting of bloodshed that cuts through the humid morning air. My nose knew the truth before my eyes saw the damage.
I pulled back and saw her ear. My dad had ripped the earring straight through the lobe because someone had asked her to dance. She didn't cry. She didn't even flinch. She just told me to go get the bag. We always had a bag. It was the only thing in our lives that stayed packed.
But something in me had shifted. I wasn't a baby in a six-year-old’s skin; I was a grown man in a boy’s frame. The trauma had hyper-accelerated my mind, turning my thoughts into the cold, hard steel of a veteran, even while my emotions stayed stagnated—locked in the dark where they couldn't grow. I looked at her and told her it was no use. He’ll find us, I said. He always finds us. I was tired of the running. I was tired of the hope that always ended in a bruise.
But Mama told me about Milton Cove. She said it was already rented. To me, Milton Cove sounded like a fortress on the moon. I trusted her because she was the only truth I had.
Even there, the play was gone. Other kids looked for toys; I looked for the Oldsmobile. I looked for the way the light hit a stranger's car, scanning for the threat. My mind was ancient, but my heart was frozen.
Then came Joe Lemair. He was a grave digger—hard-muscled and quiet, a man who spent his weekends brawling just to feel the sting of it. To a boy who had been hunted, Joe was the first breath of air I’d ever taken. At seven, I finally stepped out into the sun. I didn't have to hide in the grass anymore. I had found a shield.
But a shield only protects the outside.
By the time I was nine, I had started a war with the boy in the mirror. Every night, while the rest of the world was dreaming, I’d look at my reflection and see a coward. I’d see the boy who stood by while his mama’s blood hit the floor. It didn't matter that he was a giant. It didn't matter that I was small. The logic of a broken heart is a cruel thing, and a hyper-accelerated mind is a dangerous weapon when it’s turned inward.
I began to hate the face looking back at me. I made a promise to the dark: if I ever felt that fear again, I would burn it out with violence. I would never be the boy in the grass again.
I was nine years old, staring at my own eyes in the glass, wondering if it would be easier if I just stopped breathing. My mind was old enough to plan it, but my heart was too young to know how to heal it. The silence in that bathroom was older than the world.

## CHAPTER FIVE: THE DARKNESS AND THE LIGHT

When death becomes a serious conversation you have with yourself before you’re old enough for puberty, innocence doesn't just leave. It’s executed.
By nine years old, I was a square peg in a world that was aggressively round. I was an outsider among outsiders. I had reached a point where I realized that no one—not even the people who saw the bruises or the blood—could grasp the architecture of the shadows inside my head. I was walking in a darkness so thick that anyone else's guidance felt like static. How can someone standing in the light tell a boy in a cave which way to turn? You just end up bumping into the walls.
I became ultimately isolated. I learned to wear a mask, a cold-as-stone shield designed to hide the fact that I felt utterly worthless. I acted interested in what people had to say, but it was a lie. I was just calculating how to get around them, how to get to the next chapter, how to find a way to make it through. I didn't need anyone's opinion. The only opinion that mattered was the one in the mirror—and that man was my enemy.
Every night, I had to talk myself out of dying.
I knew where the gun was. I knew how to use it. My young mind started whispering that it would be so much easier to just eat a bullet than to carry the weight of what I was. I was at war with the reflection. I hated my body, I hated my face, and most of all, I hated the boy who felt like a coward.
That hatred fed a monster. Every time I saw that "coward" in the glass, I got more violent. I went the extra mile, begging for someone to test me, praying for a reason to prove just how not terrified I was. I was a scared little boy playing a lethal game of pretend, hoping the violence would finally drown out the shame.
The only thing that stopped me wasn't hope or a change of heart. It was the heavy, traditional weight of the fear of God. I was convinced that if I killed myself, I’d go straight to hell. I was already living in a cage, and the only reason I didn't pull the trigger was because I was terrified of what was waiting on the other side of the gunsmoke.
I stayed alive because I was too afraid to die. I was a prisoner of my own mind, waiting for a visitation I didn't even know was possible.

## CHAPTER SIX: THE CHAMELEON

Survival has a way of turning a boy into a ghost.
I was thrust from the quiet of the country into the middle of the hood, right when the air was beginning to change. Back then, the street culture that’s piped into every suburban living room today didn't exist for us. Rap wasn't a common thing yet; it was just hip-hop, a distant sound from a world I didn't know. I was just a country white boy, dropped into an environment that didn't have a slot for me.
I had a choice: adapt or be conquered. And after everything I had already been through, I wasn't about to be conquered.
So, I started shifting. I became a chameleon. I learned the cadences, the rhythms, and the slang of the streets until they felt like a second skin. At one point, I had three or four different accents I could pull from the repertoire at a moment's notice. If I was in the country, I sounded like the dirt. If I was in the hood, I sounded like the concrete.
I did it because I didn't fit in anywhere. I was a man without a country, building a house out of borrowed words. I wasn't being fake; I was being tactical. If you don't have a safe place, you make yourself look like the wall you're standing against. You hide in plain sight so the predators don't see the "coward" you still think is living under your ribs.
I spent my days perfecting the art of the blend. I was the ultimate outsider, so I became everyone else. I learned to speak everyone's language so no one would realize I didn't have a tongue of my own. I was building a masterpiece of a mask, brick by brick, slang by slang, until even I started to forget what the boy in the mirror actually sounded like.
I was safe from the world, but I was becoming a stranger to myself.

## CHAPTER SEVEN: THE FIRE AND THE CRASH

But then came the parking lot.
Away from the mirrors and the doctors, I stood in the dirt of a South Side lot and saw the atmosphere break. I stood at the end of that line, holding my mama’s hand. In that moment, her hand was the only thing that kept me grounded in reality. It was like the pillar of creation for me—the only thing holding my young mind together while the world around me dissolved into something I couldn't explain. I was terrified of the things I was seeing that my mind couldn't understand, but I was desperate for something that wasn't a needle.
When it was finally my turn, I stepped out of the shadows and stood before that man. He reached out. He touched me.
And in that moment, the world didn't just change; it split.
The details of that day would eventually blur into a fragment of a dream that didn't fit the "crash out" life I was building, but two things remained permanently burned into my mind. First, I knew God was real. I knew He existed because whatever touched me in that dirt was greater than me, and it was greater than the darkness that had been eating me alive. Second, the voices were gone. The conversation I had been having with death every night—the urge to eat a bullet and end the weight—it vanished. In the span of a single prayer, the suicidal grip was broken.
But as that darkness left, it made room for a hatred I had never known.
I was a grown man in a nine-year-old’s body, and my mind began to work with a cold, jagged logic. I looked at the miracle and I found the poison in it. I thought: If He could fix me that fast, why didn't He help my dad? Why did my mama have to spend six years being tortured while I watched? If He’s that powerful, why did we have to suffer at all?
I made my mind up in that dirt lot. I decided that if He could have stopped the fire and He didn't, then He wasn't a good God. He was an evil one.
I didn't just walk away from Him; I went on a mission to prove He was the enemy. I wasn't an atheist; I was an adversary. I hated Him because I felt like I shouldn't have had to go through that. I could have still been a kid. I could have been a baby instead of a man trapped in a small, scarred frame, trying to understand things like sex and violence before I even hit puberty.
I left that parking lot no longer wanting to kill myself, but I left it wanting to kill the reputation of God. I was God’s enemy—not metaphorically, not like a character in a book, but literally.
I had been saved from the bullet, only to spend the next thirty years trying to shoot the King.

## CHAPTER EIGHT: THE WEIGHT AND THE WEAPON

After that parking lot, I stepped into a new kind of silence. I had a mind that had been hyper-accelerated by trauma, and a heart that was desperately searching for something to love, something to defend. But the world is a cold place for a boy looking for an anchor. I threw myself into relationships, crashing out for anyone just to prove I was the most loyal, the most loving. I just wanted acceptance. But people saw that hunger in me and used it. They manipulated me, took what they could, and then abandoned me with the same tired lie: "It’s not your fault, it’s me." The abandonment didn't make me sad; it made me dangerous.
My brother Troy gave me a heavy bag, and every night for four or five years, that bag became my altar. I’d spend three or four hours straight in the heat, imagining my father’s face on the leather. I wasn't just exercising; I was practicing for a war I knew was coming. If you do anything for that long, you become great at it. I developed a precision with my hands that was deadly. I would have tested my hands against anyone.
By the time I was thirteen, the boy’s body finally caught up to the man’s mind. I grew seven inches in two years. I hit 5'11" and 175 pounds of solid "man muscle." I wasn't a kid anymore. I was formidable.
The atmosphere at home was shifting, too. My mama was with Joe Lemair, a man who had been a shield for us but could never be a father—I wasn't accepting applications for that position. Joe was a full-blown alcoholic, and when the liquor started talking reckless to my mama, I stood my ground. I couldn't whoop him yet, but I let him see it in my eyes: We both know it’s coming. Watch your mouth. I was already living a life that most adults couldn't fathom. By eleven, through a freak connection, I was moving quarter pounds of marijuana, dealing with the "plug's plug" while other kids were worried about homework. I was a child of the early 90s, when the street culture was being born in the fire of rap. I wanted to be like Tupac. I wanted the world to know I was an outsider. At thirteen, I tattooed OUTLAW across my chest.
I was a "crash out" by design. I was highly intelligent, but I didn't give a damn about the rules or the consequences. I didn't even care what my mama said—I was going to do what I wanted to do, and if the world didn't like it, the world could bleed for it.
Every time I felt fear, I didn't hide anymore. I ran at it. I met every shadow with a fist, and for a while, it worked. When you run at life that violently, things tend to bend and fold out of your way. I was a crash dummy, a dangerous weapon with an old soul, waiting for a collision that would finally be big enough to break me.

## CHAPTER NINE: THE OUTLAW AND THE SACRIFICE

By the time the eleventh year of my life settled into my bones, the war was no longer a thing of distance or noise; it had become a rhythm in my blood. I began to inhabit the skin of an outlaw, drawn to a frequency that shivered through the airwaves—a rhythmic, alien rebellion that gave a name to the violence vibrating in the deep places of my chest. I was a misfit, a Warrior born into a world of civilians, caught in the pull of a tide I didn't yet understand.
This was the reason the church felt like a room with no air. They were trying to feed me the image of a soft shepherd, a gentle guide who—while speaking the truth—was trying to lead a Warrior through a war that was being fought internally, externally, and mentally all at once. ## A Note from the King

*Locked verbatim, 2026-08-30. Not to be altered.*

I'm proud of myself today.

I need you to understand how rare that is. I've been proud of surviving things. I have never once been proud of something I chose.

There's a girl. She's eighteen. I'm forty-six. She's pregnant, she had nowhere to go, nothing to eat, and she stayed at my house.

I'm going to be honest about what that was, because if I'm not, the rest of this doesn't mean anything. She's gorgeous. She was as vulnerable as a person gets. I could have taken full advantage of her and it would have been easy. Nine men out of ten would have. I'd like to believe those are lesser men. I don't actually know that.

And I wanted to.

Something in me wouldn't let me. That's why I'm proud. But probably not for the reason you think.

Because what I wanted her for wasn't her body. That was there — I'm not going to pretend it wasn't. But underneath it was something worse. What I wanted was to put something in the hole Angel left. The hole everybody leaves. Everybody who has ever been in my life has left one, except my mother and about three other people. It's the emptiness that shows up when everybody inevitably goes. I wanted to fill it with her.

And here's how I knew I was actually helping her instead of using her: I knew that if I did it right, she'd be gone.

Because my life is not what's best for her. She's eighteen, she's pregnant, and she's got nothing. I am not what that girl needed. God is what she needed. I spent two days trying to get that into her.

So I clothed her and I fed her and I gave her a door that locked, and I did it out of the love God put in me, because there wasn't any of my own available. My flesh wanted that situation. God lived through me for two days instead.

That's what I'm proud of. It's a godly pride — I'm not proud of what I did, I'm proud of what He's doing in me. And it gave me something I don't think I've ever had. If I've had it, I haven't had it enough to recognize it.

So what's the note? What is He trying to show me?

That I'm not as wretched as I thought? No. That can't be it. Apart from Him I am exactly as wretched as I thought. Without Him I'd have taken that girl and told myself a story about it afterward. I already knew there was nothing good in me. That's not new information.

Is it that when we kill the flesh and let Him live through us, we get things we thought were impossible? That's good. That's close.

Or is it that when we kill the flesh and let Him live through us, somebody else gets the benefit and we don't?

That's it.

So many of us come to God for what we're going to get out of it. Maybe the point is that serving Him doesn't pay you. It pays you later — no, that's not true either. It does pay you. Just never in the currency you asked for.

It didn't pay me financially. It didn't fill the hole. Angel is still not here and my heart is still fucking broken and I still desperately want somebody to love me, and none of that moved an inch.

It filled different holes. I know I'm not by myself. I know He's here.

But mostly, it just didn't pay me as much as it paid her.

Because for the first time in her life she had somewhere safe. For the first time in her life somebody gave her something and didn't ask for anything back. For the first time in her life she saw what God's love actually looks like.

That's it. That's it. That's it.

When we get out of the way and let Him live, people get to see it. And the ones who see it clearest are the ones with nothing — because they don't have their religion in the way, they don't have their opinions in the way. They're just vulnerable and they need help.

The world knows that. The world is counting on it. People in the flesh look at somebody with nothing and see an opening.

God looks at the same person and doesn't charge them.

If they decide to bury themselves in hell, He will love them the whole way there. He'll love them all the way to the door.

And He'll come get you after. If you'll love Him back — if you'll just turn around and hand it back to Him, wherever you are — He'll come.

## A Word on Loyalty and Love

*Dictated 2026-09-08. Dressed for the page.*

A word on loyalty, and on love, and on the particular way a misfit gets hurt by both.

Loyalty and love, in us, aren't always a strength. Sometimes they're the door we leave open for our own abuse.

The people who love hardest and stay longest get hurt the worst, because we're the ones who stay when we should have left. We hold on when everyone else already let go. We tolerate what nobody should have to tolerate — maybe out of something misguided, maybe out of something divine, I genuinely don't know which — a need to prove we're worthy of receiving the very thing we keep giving away. And that need is exactly what gets used against us. It's the thing people learn they can lean on. We're always the one who shows up. We're always the one still holding on, because somewhere in us we want, more than anything, for one person to hold on to us the same way back.

Some of us have been lucky. We've known short seasons of real love. Some of us have even found a place — a room, a person, a people — where we weren't the outsider, where we could finally set the guard down and take the mask off.

A lot of us never get that lucky. A lot of us walk through this whole life alone — some by choice, out of the fear that all anybody will ever do is use us and then throw us away; some because we've been hurt so many times we turned into a recluse without ever deciding to. There's a thin line between those two, and most of us are standing closer to it than we'd like to admit.

If it weren't for the love of Christ, I would hate this world. What's actually in me — what I'd have become without Him — would have surfaced by now.
